Overview

The agent orchestrates the task.

Agents built on general-purpose multimodal models now handle the task level through tool calls: they capture images, segment objects, plan multi-step tasks and recover when a step fails.

The agent hands over contact to a policy.

Such an agent generalizes across layouts and instructions but executes contact poorly, whereas a learned visuomotor policy executes contact well.

The agent takes over and finishes the task.

A policy is reliable only inside its handoff boundary.

A policy completes its command reliably only from a limited range of states, and the agent may hand over outside this range. We refer to this range as the handoff boundary of the policy.

Outside it, the handoff often fails.

    More results

    The agent and the policy complement each other.

    Memory and open-vocabulary tasks rely on the agent: the whole-task policy π0.5 rarely succeeds, whereas AGP, an agent without a learned policy, performs much better on both. AGP achieves only 10.0% on the generalization subset, since its tasks test generalization to new scenes through diverse physical interactions with objects. By combining the two, our system leads in all three subsets, with the agent handling task-level reasoning and orchestration and the policy providing closed-loop control for physical interaction with objects.

    Success rates on RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboDojo

    A whole-task policy can complete most tasks in clean scenes (C2C), where π0.5 alone reaches 85.8% and Harness VLA reaches 96.0%, but randomization lowers them to 40.0% and 72.0%, whereas the success rate of our system does not drop.

    On RoboTwin 2.0, data collection and exploration use clean scenes, and evaluation uses clean (C2C) or randomized (C2R) scenes. Gen., Mem. and Open denote the generalization, memory and open-vocabulary subsets. Shaded columns are the main results: the harder C2R setting and the task-weighted average (Avg.). †Reported by the official release.

    The policy is reliable near its training data.

    We evaluate our policy on 147 subtasks of physical interaction with objects after boundary-explicit training. Starting from training poses, the policy reaches 93.2% in clean scenes and 89.8% in randomized scenes, so it generalizes well across scene appearance. Furthermore, starting from new poses, namely held-out start poses and their shifts by up to 4 cm, its success rate stays high at 83.7%.

    Both the language model and the reasoning effort of the agent affect system performance.

    The four other language models reach 67.7% to 85.0% without retraining the policy, compared with 98.7% for Claude Opus 5.5. For Claude Opus 5.5, low effort lowers the API cost per episode from $1.53 to $0.88 but also lowers the success rate to 80.3%, whereas max effort reaches 99.0%, only 0.3 percentage points above high effort, at more than twice the cost.

    We report the success rate (%) on the 30-task subset of RoboTwin 2.0 (C2R), with 10 episodes per task, and the API cost per evaluation episode at official list prices (USD).

    A closer look at agent-direct control

    Before building our system, we let an agent built on a general-purpose multimodal model control a dual-arm robot directly on RoboTwin 2.0 and RoboDojo without any robot training, which we call agent-direct control.

    What the agent does well

    The agent is strong at task-level decisions without robot training. On RoboDojo, it passes 62.5% of the open-vocabulary configurations on the first attempt but none of the precision configurations.

    Where the agent falls short

    Fewer than half of the failed trials are attributed to the agent itself. The failures of the agent itself mostly lie in physical interaction with objects.

    Most of the time goes to language-model inference. Language-model inference takes 93.0% of the total time on RoboTwin 2.0 and 86.5% on RoboDojo.

    Discussion and future directions

    • Agent-direct control is general but weak at physical interaction with objects.how to combine reasoning with learned control
    • Agent-direct control is deliberate but slow.where to spend its reasoning
    • Agent-direct control needs no training but does not learn from experience by itself.how to turn its experience into ability
    • Agent-direct control follows language, but existing benchmarks do not evaluate it well.how to evaluate it

    Among the four research directions above, this paper mainly addresses the first, how to combine reasoning with learned control, and hands physical interaction with objects over to a learned policy.

    We believe that agents for robotics are a promising path toward general physical intelligence.
